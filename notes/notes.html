Why time travel is possible, but...

If you could track the exact current state of every atom, and if we imagine a computer with enough processing power to calculate that system, the state of all matter would be sufficient to determine its future evolution through physical interactions.

In that sense, predicting the future would be possible by simulating how every atom influences every other atom. Likewise, reconstructing the past would also be possible by reversing these interactions, since the present state is the result of a continuous chain of causal events originating from the beginning of the universe.

But:

A perfect prediction of the future must include the physical process of an observer receiving and reacting to that prediction, since this reaction is itself part of the future being predicted. Because that reaction is part of the predicted future, the system can determine which later events remain unaffected within the relevant causal time window.

That reaction propagates through spacetime at finite speed, expanding its influence outward. Because of this, the visible future is progressively reduced toward the observer, with the invisible region expanding outward in proportion to that influence, over time.

The visible future recedes in proportion to your influence on it.

— Marian Dreher

Which also means: you cannot change the future by obtaining knowledge from the prediction.

A more theoretical view: because the prediction already contains the reaction — since that reaction is part of the predicted future — this isn't, in theory, a paradox. The output of the prediction could cover the future of all space and time, but that's not something the human brain can actually imagine.
